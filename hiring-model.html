Homepride Roofing — Hiring Model

James Price · Homepride Roofing Services Ltd · 1 September 2026 · edit the inputs, the results update
What this is for

Some contractors do not have a lead problem. They have a labour problem. They are fully booked, turning work away, and cannot grow until they hire. Selling those businesses more customers makes things worse.

This models a recruitment campaign the same way the client money model handles a lead campaign — spend at the top, hires at the bottom, and the revenue those hires unlock. The numbers below are editable and the results recalculate as you type.

Read This Before You Quote Anything From This Page
His filed accounts do not support what he told us

James told Ranmar he does 40–50 roofs a month and wants three or four more roofers. Homepride Roofing Services Ltd (13017629) filed micro-entity accounts to 30 November 2025 showing something quite different.

Average employees 0 (also 0 the year before) Current assets £30 (£736 the year before) Creditors due < 1 year £56,774 Fixed assets £23,625 Net assets −£33,119 (−£28,080 the year before) Turnover not disclosed — micro-entity filings never show it

Net assets are negative and got worse. £23,625 of fixed assets plus £30 of current assets against £56,774 of creditors. On the balance sheet the company is insolvent, and it was insolvent the year before too.

Zero employees is not necessarily alarming — UK roofing runs on self-employed subcontractors under CIS, and a firm can turn over a lot with nobody on payroll. It does mean “hiring employees” probably means “finding subbies”, which is a different job with different economics.

£30 of current assets at year end is the number that matters. A recruitment campaign only pays off if he can then pay the people it finds. If cash is the real constraint, more roofers makes the problem worse, not better — exactly the way selling more leads to a firm that cannot deliver makes things worse.

So do not present this model as a forecast. Present it as the arithmetic, and ask the three questions below first.

The three questions that decide whether any of this is real
  • Does he trade through this company, or another one? A firm doing 40–50 roofs a month does not hold £30 at year end. There may be a second entity, or he may run work personally.
  • Employees or subcontractors? If it is subbies, the “hire” is a day-rate arrangement, not a salary, and the cost of a bad one is a week rather than a year.
  • What does a roofer actually add per month? This is the one number in the model we do not have, and it drives the entire bottom line.

The critical difference from the lead model: a hire is not a one-off sale. A lead campaign stops paying the day you stop spending. A hire keeps producing every month they stay, which is why the money line is expressed as capacity unlocked rather than revenue this month, and why the payback usually happens inside the first job.

Where The Default Numbers Come From

Cost per application

$20 – $60

Meta lead-form applications for skilled trades. Cheaper than a customer lead, because the audience is far larger and the intent is lower.

Application → reachable

35% – 65%

The biggest leak in trade recruiting. Wrong numbers, people who applied to forty jobs, and candidates who never answer. Speed of first contact matters more than anything else here.

Screen show-up

50% – 75%

Substantially worse than a customer appointment. Reminders and same-day contact are the difference between the bottom and top of this range.

Trial → hired

30% – 60%

For skilled roles a paid trial day beats an interview. You find out in six hours what an interview will not tell you in an hour.

Revenue a hire adds

varies enormously

Ask the contractor, do not guess. The right question is “what would one more productive crew member let you invoice in a month?”

Cost per hire, all in

$400 – $2,500

What the model usually lands on for skilled trades. Compare against a recruitment agency, which typically charges a percentage of first-year salary.

These are working benchmarks from home-services and trades campaigns, offered as starting points for a conversation. They are not guarantees, they are not drawn from a published study, and they will move with the role, the market, the pay on offer and the season. Always replace them with the contractor's own figures as soon as you have them.

How To Use This On A Call
The four questions that fill it in
  • “How many people do you need, and by when?” — sets the timeline and tells you whether one campaign is enough.
  • “What would one more productive crew member let you invoice a month?” — this is the revenue input, and it is the number the whole model turns on.
  • “What is your gross margin on that work?” — turns capacity into profit.
  • “What have you tried, and what did it cost?” — job boards, agencies and word of mouth all have a price, and it is usually higher than they think.

Then run the conservative column first. If it works on conservative assumptions, the case is made without anybody having to believe an optimistic number.

The argument that lands hardest

A recruitment agency typically charges a percentage of first-year salary for one placement, and you own nothing afterwards. A campaign costs the same or less, produces a pipeline rather than a placement, and the applicants who were nearly right are still on your list next time.

And the timing argument is usually the close. Trained and productive by a given month means starting two months earlier than people expect — interviews, notice periods and training all sit in between. Walk it backwards on the call.

Fully booked is not a good problem. It is a ceiling.

Model the hire, not the lead. For a contractor turning work away, this is the only campaign that actually helps.
